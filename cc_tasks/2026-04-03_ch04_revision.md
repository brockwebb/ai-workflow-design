## Evidence Package

### E1: Absolute vs. Comparative Judgment — Literature Contradicts Current Claim

The chapter currently states: "Zero-shot numeric rating, where you ask a model 'rate this response on a scale of 1 to 5,' produces unreliable output because LLMs are poorly calibrated for absolute judgments" and "Comparative judgments are more reliable from LLMs than absolute ratings."

**The literature does not support these blanket claims.**

- Tripathi et al. (2025, arXiv:2504.14716) found pairwise protocols are MORE vulnerable to "distracted evaluation" — LLMs are systematically biased toward assertive, verbose, and sycophantic responses in pairwise mode. Pairwise preferences flipped in ~35% of cases when distractor features were added, vs. only ~9% for absolute scores.
- Absolute scoring with rubrics, few-shot examples, or logprob weighting substantially improves reliability (Wolfe, 2024 survey of LLM-as-a-judge literature).
- A 2025 ordinal-scale study found the gap between pointwise and listwise methods shrinks substantially when absolute scoring uses a fine-grained enough scale — the advantage is "coarse absolute scoring is too lossy," not "relative is inherently better."
- The BT-sigma work (arXiv:2602.16610) shows LLM pairwise probabilities can violate global ranking consistency and require reliability-aware aggregation.
- Author's own pragmatics lab notes: pairwise comparison confused verbosity for knowledge; models that correctly said "I don't have enough info" were scored lower — exactly the "distracted evaluation" pattern Tripathi et al. formalized.

**Corrected framing:** Both methods have failure modes. The choice depends on task design:
- Pairwise works when the rubric is fuzzy and you need rankings, but is vulnerable to surface-feature gaming (verbosity, assertiveness, confidence-as-proxy)
- Absolute works when you can specify explicit criteria, and is more robust to distractor features, but is less stable without rubrics
- DiGiuseppe et al.'s success with pairwise + Bradley-Terry is real, but it's a task-design win, not evidence of a universal principle

### E2: NotebookLM Mind Maps ≠ Knowledge Graphs — Confirmed

Google itself calls the feature "Mind Maps." The output is hierarchical, tree-like, with implicit "is subtopic of" relations. No ontology, no typed relationships, no entity resolution, no queryable graph substrate, no SPARQL/Cypher interface. Per Perplexity verification: "best described as a source-grounded concept map or interactive mind map, with some graph-like behavior in the UI."

Knowledge graphs require: typed entities and relations, formal schema/ontology, machine-queryable structure, entity resolution across sources, and support for inference.

**This goes in the chapter as a universalized example** (no company named): "Tools that generate hierarchical concept maps from documents and label the output a 'knowledge graph' are performing confidence laundering at the terminology level."

### E3: Model Performance Variation in Extraction — Strongly Supported

- A study of 18 LLMs on medical record extraction found accuracy ranging from 0.995 (best) to well below 0.742 (baseline RoBERTa), with substantial variation across model families (PMC/11751965).
- McGregor (2025) found a 4B parameter inflection point for extraction — below it, models fail; above it, bigger doesn't reliably mean better.
- Critically: interactions between models (e.g., embedding model choice in RAG) can cause a top-performing model to fail entirely at producing valid output.
- Author's Concept Mapper experience: GPT-4o-mini produced poor results where other models succeeded on the same extraction task.

These patterns are developed further in Chapter 12 (disclosure review, Five Safes). ### 2. Fix Absolute vs. Comparative Judgment Claim (E1)

**In the "Scaling Open-Ended Survey Responses" section, replace the current blanket claims.**

Remove: "Zero-shot numeric rating, where you ask a model 'rate this response on a scale of 1 to 5,' produces unreliable output because LLMs are poorly calibrated for absolute judgments."

Replace with something like: "Zero-shot numeric rating without explicit criteria produces unreliable output — not because LLMs are inherently incapable of absolute judgment, but because the task gives them no anchor. With a detailed rubric and scoring criteria, absolute rating improves substantially. DiGiuseppe et al. chose pairwise comparison because their construct (interest-rate knowledge) resists simple rubric definition — comparative judgment sidesteps the calibration problem for constructs where 'better' is easier to specify than 'how good.'"

Remove: "Comparative judgments are more reliable from LLMs than absolute ratings."

Replace with something like: "Both approaches have failure modes. Pairwise comparison reduces calibration burden but introduces its own biases: LLMs in comparative mode tend to favor verbose, assertive, or confident-sounding responses over concise, accurate ones. A model that correctly declines to answer — the right response when information is insufficient — can score lower than one that confabulates confidently. The choice between absolute and comparative judgment is a design decision, not a universal hierarchy."

Add citation: Tripathi et al. (2025) for the distractor-feature finding. Add BibTeX entry.

### 3. Add Model-Matters Design Principle (E3)

**In the design patterns section, add a new principle** (after "Dual-model extraction with disagreement analysis"):
